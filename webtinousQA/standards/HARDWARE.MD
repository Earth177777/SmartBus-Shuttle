## 1. Scope and accepted design

Establish the device purpose, environment, users, operating limits, safety considerations, supported host/module combinations, service model, and validation expectations. Continue established hardware and firmware choices. New processors, power systems, connectors, buses, mechanical interfaces, and manufacturing approaches require appropriate project decisions before adoption.

This standard does not mandate a smart battery, flight tracker, modem, removable module, lithium chemistry, USB-C, I2C, tool-less latch, black PCB, or a particular pinout. Earlier reference designs are candidate project profiles, not universal interfaces or validated production designs.

## 2. Architecture and interface contract

**Identify applicable hosts, modules, power sources/sinks, passive parts, controllers, communications, and external connections.** Define who controls each function and the behavior when a component is missing, unknown, unpowered, incompatible, or faulty.

For each interface, document mechanical dimensions and keying, connector/pinout, voltage and logic levels, current limits, timing/protocol, power role, identification, supported capabilities, environmental limits, and revision. Use separate interface classes where one common connector would constrain or endanger another device.

Never silently change a pinout, voltage behavior, power role, mechanical keying, or communication layer. Introduce a defined revision and compatibility plan for breaking changes.

## 3. Mechanical and industrial design

**Design enclosure, board, connector, thermal, antenna, and service access together.** Prefer clear orientation, appropriate retention, protected contacts, suitable tolerances, and a deliberate release action where removal could be harmful.

Electrical contacts should not unintentionally carry structural loads or provide the only alignment mechanism. Validate insertion force, wear, mating cycles, ingress, vibration, and accidental release according to the intended use.

A flush enclosure, hidden fasteners, minimal wiring, or tool-less attachment can be appropriate design goals. They must not override electrical correctness, safety, thermal behavior, accessibility, repairability, RF performance, or manufacturability.

## 4. Power and battery safety

Define supported sources, sinks, voltage ranges, peak/continuous loads, regulation, sequencing, charging, grounding, and fault behavior using the selected components' specifications. Do not assume a battery rail is a regulated supply. Prevent uncontrolled sources from being connected together.

Battery and power protection must be appropriate to the actual cell, charger, protection circuitry, and application. Fundamental safety must not depend only on application firmware or a working communication link. Determine required over/undervoltage, current, short-circuit, charging, and temperature protections from the design and qualified review.

Do not copy universal charge limits, trace widths, contact ratings, temperature cutoffs, or a candidate pinout into a new design without validation. A draft schematic or software simulation does not establish safe physical operation.

## 5. Communication, discovery, and firmware

**Select buses and protocols according to bandwidth, electrical conditions, distance, power, reliability, and compatibility.** A low-speed battery link is not automatically suitable for a high-speed modem.

Where modules are removable, account for partial insertion, bounce, ESD, unpowered devices, bus recovery, and source conflicts. Establish a safe discovery sequence: attachment, power stability, presence, identification, capability and compatibility checks, then controlled enablement. Unknown modules should fail safely.

Document firmware identity, supported interface revisions, update and recovery behavior, configuration, and relevant device security. Detection or acknowledgement must not be reported as proof that a physical action succeeded.

## 6. Hot-plug, hot-swap, and telemetry

**Hot-plug describes safe insertion/removal while energized.** Hot-swap additionally requires continued operation during replacement. Specify which is supported and test the distinction. A removable battery alone does not guarantee uninterrupted operation; the host may need an independently designed hold-up source.

Telemetry such as charge, health, voltage, current, temperature, runtime, or fault history is conditional on the hardware and product requirement. State units, accuracy, calibration, update cadence, unavailable states, and estimation limits. Status indicators must remain understandable without color alone where relevant.

## 7. Manufacturing and serviceability

**Select parts for electrical suitability, safety, availability, lifecycle, assembly, power, cost, and size.** Provide useful test points and inspection access. Progress from architecture prototypes to integrated prototypes and production revisions using explicit evidence and acceptance criteria.

Document assembly, programming, test, disassembly, replaceable parts, service limits, and disposal considerations where relevant. Do not equate a successful prototype with a manufacturing-qualified or regulatory-approved product.

## 8. Verification and handoff

Maintain the actual architecture, BOM, drawings, pinouts, power budgets, firmware/source identity, compatibility matrix, limitations, and test procedures. Record simulated, bench, environmental, endurance, and production checks separately.

Validate relevant power/fault behavior, interface compatibility, communication recovery, mechanical fit, thermal limits, and service procedures using the actual design. State what remains untested and what needs qualified review. Production claims require the corresponding physical and process evidence.

## Practical examples and reference

### Interface review record

| Area | Define and verify as applicable |
| --- | --- |
| Mechanical | Envelope, orientation/keying, retention, tolerances, wear, and service access. |
| Electrical | Pinout, voltage ranges, logic levels, source/sink roles, current, grounding, and protection. |
| Communication | Protocol, timing, discovery, compatibility, disconnect/recovery, and unpowered behavior. |
| Firmware | Identity, update authenticity, configuration, rollback/recovery, and fault response. |
| Validation | Source/design review, simulation, bench tests, physical tests, and manufacturing checks. |

Do not promote a candidate interface into an ecosystem contract until its design and appropriate validation are established.

### Reference-profile boundary

An earlier smart-battery concept described a removable battery, digital telemetry, and a candidate low-speed interface. Those ideas remain useful as project-specific reference material in the original archive. They do not select a cell, pinout, charger, cutoff, connector, or safe hot-swap design for a different device.

When a project adopts such a profile, put its actual specifications and evidence in that project's design record. Keep illustrative estimates distinct from selected component limits and measured behavior.

### Evidence gate

A host detecting a module establishes detection. It does not prove full current capacity, safe charging, fault protection, thermal performance, hot-swap continuity, or regulatory acceptance. Each relevant claim needs its own evidence and suitable review.

## Acceptance checklist

- **Contract:** Mechanical, electrical, communication, and power-role requirements are explicit.
- **Compatibility:** Revisions and supported combinations are traceable.
- **Safety:** Relevant protection and failure behavior are designed and appropriately reviewed.
- **Physical evidence:** Simulated, bench, physical, and production checks are distinguishable.
- **Handoff:** Actual design sources, BOM, firmware, procedures, and limitations are available.
