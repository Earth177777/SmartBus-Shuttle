## Part A — Interface and interaction quality

### 1. Product and platform fit

**Design around the task, audience, device capabilities, and established product conventions.** Reuse the existing design system. For a new interface, resolve substantial choices such as UI framework, component system, and icon family before adopting them. Webtinous identity is already selected; agree implementation details within its required design language.

A library or headless service does not need a graphical interface. Browser CSS, native controls, CLI conventions, and conversational interactions are different implementations of the same goals: clear choices, understandable state, and reliable outcomes.

### 2. Information and visual hierarchy

**Make primary tasks discoverable.** Group related information, use a logical reading order, and keep labels consistent. Use typography, spacing, alignment, and contrast to express importance. Choose scales that fit the product; pixel sizes, fonts, radii, and modal widths are project decisions rather than universal requirements.

Use product language that users understand. Explain outcomes and useful actions. Avoid unsupported claims, internal codes without explanation, placeholder content, and decorative copy that obscures the task.

### 3. Controls and forms

**Interactive controls must communicate their purpose and state through appropriate platform cues.** Prefer familiar semantics and supported native behavior where suitable.

Provide accessible names, visible labels where needed, useful examples, clear requirements, and actionable validation. Placeholders do not replace labels. Preserve valid input after recoverable errors. Avoid silently discarding work.

Distinguish primary, secondary, navigation, and destructive actions. Confirm consequential actions when required by the product's risk and permission model. Use undo where appropriate. Routine actions should not accumulate unnecessary confirmation steps.

### 4. Feedback and state

**Show an appropriate response to actions: immediate acknowledgement, progress when meaningful, and an observed success or failure.** Cover loading, empty, unavailable, permission-denied, partial-success, offline, and retry states where applicable.

Do not claim that a save, upload, payment, device action, or delivery succeeded before the authoritative result establishes it. An operation accepted for processing is different from one completed. Keep messages specific and give a useful recovery route.

Disabled controls should have understandable reasons when users need them. Avoid confusing disabled, selected, hovered, and inactive states. Long-running operations should expose cancellation or recovery when supported.

### 5. Accessibility

**Use the approved accessibility target and relevant platform guidance.** Support semantic structure, keyboard operation, visible focus, accessible names, logical reading order, scalable text, sufficient contrast, and assistive technology where applicable.

Do not communicate essential meaning through color, hover, sound, or animation alone. Provide alternatives for meaningful media. Respect reduced-motion and contrast preferences when relevant. Touch targets and spacing should meet the platform's adopted guidance.

Dialogs and overlays need appropriate focus placement, escape/close behavior, focus restoration, and usable scrolling. Critical content must remain available long enough to understand and act on it. Keep security and accessibility intact in error states.

### 6. Icons, brand, and components

**Reuse the project's accepted assets and icon system.** No library is mandated by this standard. Resolve conflicting historical guidance through the recorded project decision rather than silently mixing systems.

Give icon-only actions accessible names; do not make the icon's drawing determine the entire hit area. Keep icon meanings, weight, sizing, and alignment consistent within context. Custom and platform icons may coexist for a documented need.

Use centralized tokens and shared components where appropriate. Part B of DESIGN.MD covers identity; the required Webtinous identity applies to adopting work. Preserve accurate client/product identity alongside the required Webtinous attribution.

### 7. Responsive behavior and motion

**Support the intended viewport sizes, input methods, text lengths, and locales.** Adapt layouts at content-driven boundaries. Mobile support and multiple themes are conditional requirements, covered by DESIGN.MD and DESIGN.MD.

Motion should explain a change or guide attention. Preserve readability and control, avoid disruptive effects, and provide reduced-motion behavior. Choose durations from interaction needs; a fixed timing table is not a universal standard.

### 8. Performance and verification

**Use representative devices and datasets.** Avoid unnecessary rendering work, heavy effects, and uncontrolled polling. Introduce virtualization, caching, or a new UI dependency only when evidence and the accepted architecture justify it. Do not sacrifice accessibility or state correctness for a benchmark.

Review complete workflows, including failure and recovery, with realistic content. Check labels, focus, keyboard and touch behavior, clipping, scrolling, long text, localization, supported devices, and relevant themes. Record what was actually checked and any limits. A polished screenshot alone does not verify an interaction.

### Practical examples and reference

#### Example: a clear asynchronous workflow

| State | What the user should understand | Useful interface behavior |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ready | What action is available and any prerequisites. | Clear label and accessible control. |
| Running | The request started and whether it can be cancelled. | Immediate feedback; meaningful progress where available. |
| Accepted/pending | Work is queued but the final outcome is not known. | Honest pending state and a way to inspect progress. |
| Completed | The authoritative outcome succeeded. | Specific confirmation and the relevant result. |
| Failed/partial | What failed, what succeeded, and what can happen next. | Preserve useful input; provide safe recovery. |

Do not replace these distinctions with a generic success notification after every click.

#### Readable standards and product documentation

Use a descriptive title, a short purpose statement, meaningful heading levels, brief paragraphs, and parallel lists. Bold decisive rules and field names selectively. Use tables for mappings and comparisons, and code blocks for copyable code or configuration. Do not make an entire page bold or use decorative banners between every paragraph.

For repository documentation, use ordinary Markdown with accessible images, descriptive links, clear tables, and a short navigation list. Keep essential setup and decision rules visible. A simple banner can identify the collection without becoming part of a project's product identity.

#### Review with actual content

Inspect the longest useful labels, realistic empty/error states, permission restrictions, slow operations, keyboard focus, and supported screen sizes. A carefully composed demo screen does not replace testing the real workflow.

### Acceptance checklist

- **Clarity:** Primary tasks, current state, and meaningful next actions are understandable.
- **Semantics:** Controls have appropriate labels, roles, keyboard behavior, and focus.
- **Feedback:** Success, pending, partial, and failure states reflect actual outcomes.
- **Adaptation:** Relevant devices, text sizes, locales, and themes remain usable.
- **Evidence:** Complete workflows were reviewed with realistic content and limitations recorded.

## Part B — Brand and design system

### 1. Identity and approval

**Reuse the existing approved brand, assets, typography, icon system, and design tokens.** A new project needs an agreed identity and substantial visual choices before they are adopted. Webtinous identity and the palette in Part E are required. Icon vendors, font packages, and implementation frameworks remain project decisions; use accepted choices or resolve missing major choices first.

A small internal script or headless library may need only a clear name and documentation. Do not create logos, a design system, a website, or a graphical interface merely to adopt this standard.

### 2. Central configuration

Keep product names, approved asset references, reusable colors, typography, spacing, radii, and component state values in an appropriate shared source. This may be CSS tokens, native theme resources, a design library, or another established mechanism.

Separate brand roles from semantic roles such as success, warning, danger, information, selection, and focus. Do not make one brand color carry every meaning. Choose values for readability and the intended devices; sample sizes and palettes are proposals rather than universal constraints.

### 3. Assets and logos

**Use approved assets with known provenance and suitable rights.** Preserve proportions and appropriate clear space. Use the intended light, dark, monochrome, or small-format variant where provided. Do not arbitrarily recolor, distort, redraw, or add effects to an official mark.

Use descriptive, stable names and organize assets according to the platform's conventions. Browser favicons, app-store icons, splash screens, printed marks, and terminal branding are conditional deliverables. Do not require every format in every project.

### 4. Icons and visual language

**Use a coherent icon system.** Continue the project's accepted library or platform set. Ask before adopting a substantial new icon dependency or replacing the visual system. Custom icons and multiple sets can be appropriate for a defined need, but their styles and meanings should remain intentional.

Keep icon weight, optical sizing, alignment, and meaning consistent. Provide accessible names for functional icon-only controls. The visible symbol and the interactive target may have different dimensions. An emoji or text symbol is acceptable when it fits the platform and remains understandable; it is not a universal substitute for an accessible control.

### 5. UI scales and component states

**Choose reusable typography and spacing scales suited to the content, platform, and approved design.** Avoid scattered arbitrary values, but allow justified optical adjustments and platform constraints. Do not force a universal pixel grid, font family, or fixed button size.

Define relevant hover, focus, active, selected, loading, disabled, error, and success states. Preserve accessibility across states and supported themes. Reuse shared components rather than maintaining inconsistent screen-level copies.

### 6. Attribution and product language

**Use accurate names, ownership, copyright, licenses, and credits.** Keep product identity, studio credit, and third-party attribution distinct. Webtinous attribution is already required by these standards. A compliance badge additionally needs applicable criteria and supporting evidence.

Use clear, professional language for the intended audience. Describe actual functionality and outcomes. Avoid unsupported superlatives, fabricated statistics, placeholder claims, and unexplained internal jargon.

### 7. Review

Check approved assets, name consistency, contrast, component states, icon meaning, typography, supported devices, relevant themes, and actual output formats. Record limitations. A new brand proposal must be identified as a proposal until accepted; an established approved system can be used without asking again.

### Practical examples and reference

#### Suggested token inventory

| Group | Example roles | Review concern |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Identity | Product name, logo variants, app icon, authorized credit. | Accuracy, rights, and correct usage. |
| Color | Brand accent, background, surface, text, border, focus. | Readability and semantic consistency. |
| Status | Success, warning, danger, information, selection. | Meaning remains clear beyond color alone. |
| Typography | Body, headings, captions, code. | Legibility, fallback behavior, and supported scripts. |
| Geometry | Spacing, radius, elevation, control sizes. | Consistency and platform usability. |

These roles are examples. Use the platform's existing token mechanism and omit irrelevant groups. Native apps, web interfaces, printed material, and terminal tools do not need identical asset formats.

#### Banner and repository assets

A repository banner should identify the repository and its purpose, remain readable at narrow widths, and include meaningful alternative text. Keep essential instructions as actual text below it. Supply a light/dark variant when useful and ensure relative paths work after the repository is copied.

Use factual labels such as the standard version and applicable scope. Avoid badges that imply a security review, production readiness, or successful CI when no corresponding evidence exists. Do not copy the standards pack's banner into a product as its brand identity by default.

### Acceptance checklist

- **Source:** Approved assets and usage rights are known.
- **Tokens:** Reusable roles are centralized through an appropriate mechanism.
- **Icons:** Meanings, styles, and accessible names remain coherent.
- **Variants:** Actual delivery sizes and applicable themes are reviewed.
- **Attribution:** Product, studio, and third-party identities are distinguishable.

## Part C — Device adaptation

### 1. Supported devices

Determine the intended devices, screen/window sizes, input methods, orientations, browsers or operating systems, and accessibility requirements from the project. Preserve established support decisions. Ask before materially expanding the supported platform scope.

This standard does not require a mobile app, responsive website, new framework, or support for every device. A desktop-only tool, command-line program, headless service, or fixed embedded display can have a narrower documented support scope.

### 2. Layout and content

**Adapt to available space and content rather than arbitrary device names.** Use the project's established layout primitives and breakpoints. Keep primary tasks reachable, information hierarchy clear, text readable, and navigation understandable.

Allow content to wrap, grow, and scroll appropriately. Test long names, translations, validation messages, large datasets, and enlarged text. Avoid accidental page overflow, clipped controls, overlapping content, and fixed heights that hide essential information.

Use columns, stacked sections, progressive disclosure, or alternate representations according to the task. Do not hide critical information merely to make a narrow layout appear clean. If a feature is intentionally limited on a device, explain the limit and any supported alternative.

### 3. Interaction and navigation

**Support the actual input methods: touch, pointer, keyboard, assistive technology, or hardware controls.** Essential actions must not depend solely on hover, fine pointer precision, or a gesture with no alternative.

Size and space controls according to the adopted platform guidance and audience. Keep focus visible and actionable elements reachable. Choose navigation patterns based on task structure; a bottom navigation bar, sidebar, or floating button is not required by default.

Preserve relevant state when resizing, rotating, navigating, or moving between responsive layouts. Do not reset forms or lose selected data solely because the viewport changed.

### 4. Forms and onscreen keyboards

**Keep labels, errors, and primary actions usable when a keyboard appears.** Use appropriate input types, autofill, validation, and safe-area behavior where supported. Avoid unintended zoom, hidden focused inputs, and primary actions covered by fixed elements.

Support recoverable errors without discarding valid work. Prevent duplicate submission where it would corrupt state. A narrower layout should not change the authority or validation applied to an operation.

### 5. Dense content and overlays

**Choose a usable presentation for large tables, charts, lists, and operational dashboards.** Bounded scrolling, column priority, detail views, or alternative layouts can be appropriate. Preserve essential relationships and accessible reading order.

Dialogs, sheets, dropdowns, and popovers need usable dimensions, scrolling, focus behavior, and close/recovery paths. A bottom sheet is an optional platform pattern, not a mandatory replacement for every modal.

### 6. Device capabilities and connectivity

**Use capability detection and documented fallbacks where relevant.** Handle notches, safe areas, dynamic browser chrome, device orientation, media controls, and resource limits within the supported scope.

Offline capability is a product decision. If network operations exist, explain interruption, pending work, retry, and uncertain outcomes. Do not imply that local acknowledgement proves cloud delivery or device execution. Avoid inventing offline synchronization because mobile support was requested.

### 7. Performance and accessibility

**Test representative devices, including lower-powered targets when relevant.** Optimize media, rendering, memory, network use, and animation based on evidence. Respect reduced-motion and text scaling preferences. Keep meaningful content and control labels available to assistive technology.

Device adaptation must preserve data correctness, permissions, accessibility, and useful feedback. A visually smaller version of a desktop screen is not automatically a usable mobile design.

### 8. Verification

Review actual primary workflows, forms with keyboard open, navigation, overlays, long content, supported orientations, relevant themes, loading/error states, and network interruption. Check both touch and keyboard where supported.

Record devices or simulated viewports, environment, checks, and limitations. A browser simulation does not prove native-device behavior. Preserve the existing project scope unless the user approves a major platform expansion.

### Practical examples and reference

#### Representative review matrix

| Situation | Check |
| --- | --- |
| Narrow viewport and long text | Essential information wraps and controls remain reachable. |
| Form with keyboard open | Focused input, error, and next action remain usable. |
| Enlarged text | Reading order and important controls survive reflow. |
| Rotation or resize | Unsaved work and relevant selection are preserved. |
| Dense data | Scrolling or alternate views preserve meaning and access. |
| Interrupted request | Pending, failed, and completed outcomes stay distinguishable. |

Choose actual targets from the supported-device scope. Do not treat this matrix as a demand to support every platform or orientation.

#### A useful gate distinction

A responsive web layout checked in a simulated viewport can pass its viewport check. It does not establish that a native mobile app, on-screen keyboard, camera permission, physical scanner, or device-specific bridge works. Record those boundaries independently when they belong to the product.

For a desktop-only utility, a mobile module can be Not applicable with its supported scope stated. For a new consumer mobile workflow, device support is a real product decision and must be resolved before promising compatibility.

### Acceptance checklist

- **Targets:** The supported devices, inputs, and orientations are explicit.
- **Content:** Long content and enlarged text preserve important information.
- **Interaction:** Navigation, forms, overlays, and focus are usable on relevant targets.
- **State:** Resize, rotation, interruption, and retries preserve correct state.
- **Evidence:** Simulated and physical-device checks are labeled accurately.

## Part D — Themes and color modes

### 1. Applicability and choices

**Multiple themes are optional product capabilities.** Do not add dark mode, a theme library, preference storage, or a settings screen solely because this file is present. Continue the project's accepted theme architecture. For a new project, ask whether multiple themes are needed before making a substantial implementation choice.

A single-theme product still needs readable, accessible colors. A headless program may mark this module Not applicable. Respect native platform behavior where appropriate.

### 2. Preference and persistence

**Define the supported modes and preference precedence.** A common proposal is explicit user selection, then system preference, then product fallback; use the project's accepted behavior rather than imposing this ordering universally.

Persist choices only through an appropriate existing mechanism. Local storage, native settings, cookies, account synchronization, and no persistence each have different consequences. Do not add a backend merely to store a theme.

Where applicable, prevent distracting initial flashes, keep server/client or native state consistent, and handle system preference changes. Switching themes must preserve forms, navigation, focus, and unsaved work.

### 3. Semantic tokens

**Use reusable roles for backgrounds, surfaces, elevation, primary and secondary text, borders, focus, selection, and semantic states.** Map those roles to the supported themes through the platform's established configuration.

Define independent light and dark values where needed. Do not implement a theme only by inverting every color. Pure white or black may be appropriate in context, but neither is universally required or prohibited. Prioritize legibility and hierarchy over an assumed battery or style benefit.

### 4. Meaning and contrast

**Keep success, warning, danger, information, focus, and selection meanings stable across themes.** Adjust values to preserve usable contrast and distinguish relevant states. Essential meaning must not rely on color alone.

Evaluate text, controls, icons, charts, borders, and focus indicators against the approved accessibility target. A bright accent can still be unreadable against a particular surface. Check actual foreground/background combinations rather than assuming a palette is accessible.

### 5. Components and assets

Review inputs, buttons, links, navigation, tables, charts, dialogs, popovers, tooltips, loading indicators, disabled states, alerts, code blocks, and media controls in each supported theme.

Use appropriate approved logo and illustration variants. Maintain image and chart readability without arbitrary filters that distort product evidence or meaning. Inspect embedded and third-party content; document limits where it cannot be themed safely.

Reuse the current framework or native resource mechanism. CSS variables, data attributes, utility classes, and platform appearance APIs are possible implementations, not mandated technologies.

### 6. Motion, performance, and robustness

**Theme changes should be stable and quick.** Avoid unnecessary rerendering, duplicated state, layout shifts, or animation that delays interaction. Respect reduced-motion preferences. Essential behavior must not depend on a transition animation.

Handle first launch, absent or invalid saved preferences, system changes, private or restricted storage, sign-in/out where relevant, and multiple windows/tabs only within the supported product scope. Do not silently overwrite user choice.

### 7. Verification

Check initial appearance, preference precedence, persistence, supported navigation and component states, overlays, charts, media, accessibility, and state preservation. Review long content and supported devices. Verify actual user and system preference changes when included in scope.

Record the themes and environments checked. Testing one theme does not verify another; installing a theme package does not establish a working accessible implementation.

### Practical examples and reference

#### Theme review matrix

| Area | Review in every supported mode |
| --- | --- |
| Text and surfaces | Body, muted text, links, nested surfaces, and borders. |
| Interaction | Focus, hover, active, selected, disabled, loading, and error states. |
| Floating content | Menus, dialogs, popovers, tooltips, and notifications. |
| Data and media | Charts, images, logos, illustrations, code, and embedded content. |
| Preference lifecycle | First launch, explicit choice, system changes, persistence, and invalid saved values. |

Inspect actual combinations. Token names and successful theme toggling alone do not prove readability.

#### Preference example

If the accepted product behavior gives an explicit user choice priority over the operating system, a later system change must not silently overwrite that choice. If the user selects a system-following mode, relevant system changes should be reflected. Treat this as an example of a documented preference contract; preserve a different accepted contract where appropriate.

#### Scope gate

A project that currently has one approved theme does not acquire a dark-mode requirement by adopting this file. If another theme is requested, decide its scope, implementation, and acceptance criteria. Reuse the existing theme system when it can satisfy the need.

### Acceptance checklist

- **Scope:** Supported modes and preference precedence are agreed.
- **Tokens:** Theme roles map consistently to actual platform values.
- **Readability:** Relevant components, states, media, and charts are checked in each mode.
- **State:** Switching preserves focus, navigation, and unsaved work.
- **Persistence:** Actual preference behavior and its limitations are verified.

## Part E — Required Webtinous identity and 404 standard

### 1. Required identity

**Webtinous · by Continous Group is mandatory for adopting projects.** Reuse approved Webtinous assets and centralized brand tokens. This requirement does not select a programming language, framework, font dependency, icon vendor, hosting provider, or container system. Preserve truthful client/product names, legal notices, and third-party attribution alongside the required identity.

Use supplied, verified official logo assets. Do not distort, redraw, or invent a logo. When required assets are missing, complete independent work and identify the missing asset; use a text wordmark only if it is an accepted fallback. Required branding must not be downgraded to optional during generalization or refactoring.

| --- | --- |
| Webtinous blue | #2b57f5 |
| Webtinous purple | #9d3bf2 |
| Webtinous pink | #f03aa6 |
| Orange accent | #ff7b29 |

Use semantic theme tokens and accessible combinations. Adapt supporting surfaces/text for dark mode and contrast without arbitrarily changing the brand identity. Keep one coherent accepted icon system; do not install a vendor merely because a previous example named it.

### 2. Required Webtinous 404 experience

**Every adopting web product with routes MUST implement the Webtinous 404 experience** through its existing framework/router and component system. Apply it to not-found and unmatched routes, including direct navigation and refresh. Preserve correct HTTP 404 behavior where the server/runtime supports it; document client-only routing limitations rather than claiming the server returned 404 when it returned a fallback page.

| Required element | Content and behavior |
| --- | --- |
| Identity | Approved Webtinous logo or accepted wordmark fallback. |
| Status | Prominent 404 with a meaningful page title and heading structure. |
| Main message | “Stuck in a loop?” |
| Explanation | “The page you are looking for has vanished.” |
| Recovery | “Back to Home” leading to the actual supported home/workspace route. |
| Contact | “Still lost? Contact us” with verified, useful support/contact destinations. |
| Credit | Webtinous · by Continous Group and accurate authorized copyright/owner notices. |

Required visual language: **blue → purple → pink gradient**, a light/glass-like surface with a readable opaque fallback, a rounded card, a loop-style graphic, restrained motion, and responsive layout. In a dark theme, use an intentionally designed readable equivalent. The loop motif is decorative; the error must not misleadingly appear to be an operation still loading.

Motion must respect reduced-motion preferences and must not block recovery or essential content. Keep decorative graphics out of the accessibility reading order. Support keyboard use, visible focus, appropriate alternative text, readable contrast, text scaling, and usable touch targets. Avoid flashing, excessive animation, clipped contact links, and full-screen effects that degrade performance.

Implement the design using the project's accepted stack. Do not copy the old static HTML/CSS/JavaScript sample into every repository, weaken content security policy, add an animation dependency, or replace routing architecture to satisfy the visual standard. All contact links must use current approved project configuration; do not invent or blindly reuse old addresses. No permanent placeholder “Contacting…” state is acceptable in the release.

### 3. Other error and non-web surfaces

Use the same identity and clear recovery language for applicable permission, validation, timeout, throttling, unavailable-service, and internal-error states. Keep actual status semantics accurate. Do not expose stack traces, database details, secrets, internal paths, or private resource existence contrary to the security model.

A CLI, library, native app without web routing, or embedded device does not need an artificial web page. It still needs appropriate Webtinous attribution and clear error/help behavior through supported CLI help/version output, library documentation, native About/help, or device documentation/UI as applicable.

### 4. Credits, notices, and quality claims

Show the required identity in the appropriate footer, About/help, documentation, release notes, and package metadata where supported. Keep product identity and third-party notices accurate. Attribution does not authorize a false statement of ownership or certification.

The internal Webtinous quality designation “Credibility, Integrity, Accessibility” remains distinct from the security CIA triad. Display “Webtinous CIA Standard Compliant” only with defined criteria, an authorized review, and release-specific evidence. Branding and this document alone do not prove certification or production readiness.

### 5. Release acceptance checklist

- **Identity:** Required Webtinous by Continous Group attribution and approved assets are present.
- **404 routing:** Unknown paths, deep links, refresh, and the home/recovery destination work; status behavior is verified or its actual limitation recorded.
- **Content:** Required 404 copy, loop motif, contact section, and accurate credit appear without placeholders.
- **Presentation:** Gradient, card, surfaces, typography, relevant themes, responsive layout, and long contact text are readable.
- **Accessibility:** Keyboard, focus, text scaling, reduced motion, semantic structure, and relevant assistive technology have appropriate evidence.
- **Security:** Error states reveal no sensitive internals; branding does not weaken security controls.
- **Release record:** Screenshots/check results and unresolved constraints are recorded under webtinousQA/ and referenced by BUILD.MD's release report.
